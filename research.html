SCIENTIFIC MOTIVATION & PhD DIRECTION

How does molecular gas
become a population of stars?

The stellar initial mass function (IMF) provides a long-term scientific anchor for my research. My aim is to understand the processes that precede the stellar mass distribution: how molecular gas develops structure, how it fragments, and which physical conditions regulate star formation.

I approach star formation as a complex system in which gravity, turbulence, magnetic fields, and radiation or stellar feedback are coupled. Rather than attempting to model all of these processes at once, I aim to build physical understanding step by step, eventually working towards a more integrated description.

01 / CURRENT FOUNDATION

Quantify cloud structure

Use three-dimensional dust reconstructions to measure how cloud geometry changes with the gas-density threshold, and compare reconstructed structure with three orthogonal projections.

02 / NEXT OBSERVATIONAL STEP

Connect structure to gas physics

Bring in molecular-line measurements of gas kinematics and constraints on physical conditions to test specific explanations for the structural differences.

03 / TOWARDS SYNTHESIS

Test physical interpretations

Apply comparable diagnostics to simulations and synthetic observations to assess projection effects and distinguish plausible physical explanations before pursuing an integrated model.

Longer-term methodological interests

Discussions with my supervisor have also motivated an interest in agent-based modelling and symbolic regression as possible tools for an eventual synthesis. These are exploratory directions, rather than completed components of my current cloud-structure analysis.

MAIN PROJECT · 2023–PRESENT

Cloud geometry
across density thresholds.

Ongoing research · Manuscript in preparation

I study seven nearby star-forming regions—Cepheus, Lupus, Orion A, Orion B, λ Orionis, Perseus, and Taurus—using the three-dimensional dust reconstruction of Leike et al. (2020). I convert reconstructed extinction to hydrogen number density, nH, under an adopted dust-to-gas conversion.

Within each reconstructed region, I raise the threshold in nH to select progressively denser structure and measure its geometry. I also analyse the corresponding column-density projections in the X–Y, Y–Z, and X–Z planes. Volume-density thresholds in 3D and column-density thresholds in projected analyses are distinct quantities.

I independently implemented four families of structural diagnostics and developed the Python workflow for threshold scans, connected-structure measurements, scaling fits, and comparison plots.

These diagnostics describe complementary aspects of geometry. Their exponents are not interchangeable measurements of a single property, so a rising boundary-based exponent and a falling size-based exponent need not contradict one another.

WHAT I FIND SO FAR

A cloud’s structural description changes with the density level.

The following qualitative patterns are emerging from the current analysis. They refer to the studied regions and threshold ranges for which the diagnostics can be measured; fit selection, scale coverage, and uncertainty assessment remain part of the ongoing work.

1. Systematic, diagnostic-dependent trends

Across the analysed regions, the structural diagnostics show systematic, predominantly monotonic changes as the density threshold increases. In 3D, the surface–volume-derived dimension increases, whereas the volume–radius-of-gyration, volume–size, and box-counting dimensions decrease.

These patterns question whether one threshold-independent fractal dimension adequately summarises an entire cloud. Threshold dependence alone does not establish multifractality: changing the density threshold selects different structures, whereas a formal multifractal analysis requires additional measurements.

2. The projected trends depend on orientation

The projected diagnostics show analogous threshold-dependent behaviour. In the current analysis, the variation is stronger in X–Y projections, parallel to the Galactic plane, than in the Y–Z and X–Z projections perpendicular to it. This points to an orientation dependence of the measured cloud morphology.

3. Regions follow different threshold-dependent trajectories

In the perimeter–area analysis, Lupus and Taurus—regions associated predominantly with low-mass star formation—reach relatively high dimension estimates at lower column-density thresholds than the other regions in the sample. This suggests a possible connection between cloud geometry and differences in star-forming environments, to be tested with controlled comparisons.

PHYSICAL INTERPRETATION / OPEN QUESTION

Which processes produce these geometric changes?

The combination of trends may reflect changes in boundary structure and spatial organisation as denser gas is selected. Filamentary structure, turbulence, gravity, magnetic fields, and feedback are possible connections to investigate, rather than mechanisms identified by morphology alone.

In particular, the difference between Galactic-plane and perpendicular projections does not by itself demonstrate stronger or more complex gas motions in the plane. It motivates a test of whether the structural anisotropy is associated with anisotropy in gas kinematics, while accounting for projection, resolution, cloud geometry, and selection effects.

NEXT SCIENTIFIC STEP

Connect measured geometry to independent gas-motion information.

I want to test whether structural changes across density thresholds are associated with velocity coherence, line-of-sight velocity dispersion, or velocity gradients in molecular-line data. The first task is to define a tractable sample and establish which observables the available data can support, at matched physical resolution and scale coverage.

The dust reconstruction provides spatial distance information; a molecular-line cube provides position–position–velocity information. I will need to associate spatial structures with relevant velocity components and account for excitation, optical depth, and observational filtering, rather than treating line intensity as a direct density measurement.

Comparisons with simulations can then test how the same diagnostics respond to projection and to different physical scenarios. This extends the current geometric analysis towards my broader PhD question without assuming that morphology alone identifies a cause or demonstrates physical fragmentation.
